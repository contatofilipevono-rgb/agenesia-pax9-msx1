## Abstract

Missense variants in the DNA-binding domains of *PAX9* and *MSX1* cause non-syndromic tooth agenesis, yet most substitutions remain variants of uncertain significance. We investigated the structural mechanisms of 39 pathogenic missense variants in the PAX9 paired domain (n = 32) and MSX1 homeodomain (n = 7) alongside 77 non-coding/splice ClinVar variants. Protein–DNA complexes were modelled with AlphaFold 3, folding stability changes (ΔΔG) were estimated with ThermoMPNN, and pathogenic variants were compared with gnomAD v4 domain variants. AlphaFold 3 confidence metrics and mutant interfaces were insensitive to point mutations. Instead, pathogenic *PAX9* variants acted through two complementary mechanisms relative to recurrent population controls: loss of direct DNA contact (< 4 Å; 50% vs 17%; p = 0.0025) or domain destabilization (median ΔΔG 1.17 vs 0.34 kcal/mol; p = 4.6×10⁻⁵). Leave-one-out cross-validation selected a stable rule (DNA contact or ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol; apparent sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.79) concordant with published DNA-binding assays for five of eight *PAX9* mutants. Mechanism did not predict missing teeth, whereas missense variants were milder than null alleles (p = 0.001). AlphaGenome matched SpliceAI and jointly prioritized seven intronic variants.

**Keywords:** Anodontia; Homeodomain Proteins; Mutation, Missense; PAX9 Transcription Factor; Protein Stability

## 1. Introduction

Non-syndromic tooth agenesis—the congenital absence of one or more permanent teeth without systemic anomalies—is the most common developmental anomaly of the human dentition and has a predominantly genetic aetiology. Database surveys of non-syndromic tooth agenesis have catalogued approximately 198 causal variants across 15 genes, of which 91.9% cluster in seven core odontogenic genes: *AXIN2*, *EDA*, *LRP6*, *MSX1*, *PAX9*, *WNT10A*, and *WNT10B* (1). Within this network, *PAX9* and *MSX1* encode the two principal sequence-specific transcription factors: *PAX9* variants preferentially cause molar oligodontia affecting second permanent molars (2), whereas *MSX1* variants typically affect second premolars and maxillary first premolars or third molars (3).

During early odontogenesis, tooth morphogenesis depends on reciprocal signalling between the oral ectoderm and cranial neural crest-derived ectomesenchyme. Both *Pax9* and *Msx1* are co-expressed in the condensing dental mesenchyme, where they maintain mesenchymal *Bmp4* expression required for the transition from the bud stage to the cap stage. Homozygous deletion of either *Msx1* (4) or *Pax9* (5) in mice arrests tooth development at the bud stage. In humans, heterozygous loss-of-function variants act in a dosage-sensitive manner, and PAX9 and MSX1 physically and functionally synergize to activate the *Bmp4* and *Msx1* promoters (6). Notably, functional studies have shown that certain pathogenic substitutions—such as PAX9 G51S—preserve monomeric DNA binding while abolishing synergistic transactivation with MSX1, demonstrating that missense variants can disrupt odontogenesis through distinct molecular routes (6).

Genotype–phenotype syntheses have consolidated the clinical spectrum of both genes, including 61 *PAX9* variants across 178 patients compiled by Intarak and colleagues (2) and disease-associated *MSX1* substitutions concentrated in the homeodomain—such as R202P, T180I, and L211R—reviewed by Liang and colleagues (3, 7, 8). However, direct biochemical characterization remains available for only a small fraction of reported alleles, most notably eight paired-domain PAX9 mutants assayed by electrophoretic mobility shift and reporter transactivation by Wang and colleagues (6). Meanwhile, routine clinical sequencing has led to a rapid accumulation of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and unclassified population substitutions in ClinVar (9), leaving clinicians and geneticists without mechanism-aware criteria to prioritize coding or non-coding variants in these loci.

In structural genetics of transcription factors, disease-causing missense substitutions classically fall into two complementary categories first formalized in p53: "DNA-contact" variants that remove direct base or phosphate interactions without unfolding the domain, and "structural" variants that destabilize the protein core and abrogate DNA binding indirectly (10, 11). Applying protein-design energy calculations to the homologous PAX6 paired domain in aniridia, Alibés and colleagues (12) using FoldX (13) accounted for 44 of 50 (88%) pathogenic missense substitutions through predicted losses of folding stability or DNA binding or specificity. Subsequent high-throughput binding surveys confirmed widespread affinity and specificity perturbations across human transcription factor families, particularly paired-domain and homeodomain proteins (14, 15). Yet no systematic structural analysis has examined *PAX9* and *MSX1* variants in tooth agenesis, nor have biomolecular complex predictors such as AlphaFold 3 (16), deep-learning stability predictors trained on mega-scale proteolysis data such as ThermoMPNN (17), large population reference cohorts from gnomAD v4 (18), sequence-based pathogenicity models such as AlphaMissense (19), or long-range regulatory sequence models such as AlphaGenome (20) alongside SpliceAI (21) been evaluated in this context.

Here, we combined AlphaFold 3 protein–DNA modelling, ThermoMPNN saturation stability estimation, gnomAD v4 population controls, and AlphaGenome regulatory scoring to address four objectives: (i) to test whether pathogenic *PAX9* and *MSX1* missense variants segregate into complementary DNA-contact and domain-destabilizing mechanisms relative to population variants; (ii) to evaluate the stability of a combined structural decision rule and compare its mechanistic predictions against published DNA-binding experiments and clinical tooth-loss severity; (iii) to benchmark AlphaGenome against SpliceAI on non-coding ClinVar variants; and (iv) to provide an exploratory, research-level structural characterization of domain VUS.

## 2. Material and methods

### 2.1 Variant curation and population control definition

Single-gene variant records for human *PAX9* and *MSX1* were retrieved programmatically from ClinVar via NCBI E-utilities (9) on 15 November 2026 and mapped to canonical protein isoforms (UniProt accessions P55771 for PAX9 and P28360 for MSX1). Missense variants were cross-referenced against curated family reports in the systematic review supplement of Intarak et al. (2) for *PAX9* and Table 2 of Liang et al. (3) for *MSX1*. Population allele counts (AC) and allele frequencies (AF) across combined exomes and genomes (GRCh38) were queried via the Broad Institute GraphQL API (`dataset: gnomad_r4`) (18) on 15 November 2026; precomputed AlphaMissense pathogenicity scores were retrieved from AlphaFold Database proteome files (19). An attempted query of the Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD) failed owing to server IP blocking (HTTP 403) and was not used.

Consolidated missense variants were assigned non-overlapping labels: `P` for ClinVar Pathogenic or Likely pathogenic assertions without conflicting interpretations; `P-lit` for additional substitutions reported in families with non-syndromic tooth agenesis (2, 3) with gnomAD AF < 10⁻⁴ and no conflicting ClinVar assertion; `B` for ClinVar Benign/Likely benign variants or gnomAD AF ≥ 0.001; and `U` for ClinVar VUS, conflicting entries, or unclassified population variants. Translation initiation (`start_lost`, Met1) substitutions and one literature entry incompatible with the reference sequence ("PAX9 Asn16Ile", where position 16 in P55771 is Val) were excluded. Across full-length proteins, curation yielded 568 *PAX9* and 793 *MSX1* missense entries. Structural analyses focused on the DNA-binding domains: residues 6–130 of the PAX9 paired domain (UniProt domain annotation 4–130, bounded at residue 6 by template construct coverage) and residues 172–231 of the MSX1 homeodomain (excluding four extra-domain pathogenic candidates: PAX9 Y143C and A168G; MSX1 G122E and P247S).

The final pathogenic domain set comprised 39 missense variants: 32 in *PAX9* (16 ClinVar `P` and 16 `P-lit`) and 7 in *MSX1* (2 ClinVar `P` and 5 `P-lit`). Population control sets comprised all gnomAD v4 missense variants within the same domain boundaries lacking `P` or `P-lit` labels, evaluated both across all observed alleles (AC ≥ 1: *PAX9*, n = 124; *MSX1*, n = 59) and restricted to recurrent population variants (AC ≥ 2: *PAX9*, n = 48; *MSX1*, n = 29, primary calibration control). All 124 *PAX9* controls and 58 of 59 *MSX1* controls (all except Likely benign S210N, AC = 143) carried the `U` label and therefore overlapped with the domain VUS catalogue (*PAX9*, n = 138; *MSX1*, n = 72).

### 2.2 Biomolecular complex modelling with AlphaFold 3

Wild-type and mutant protein–DNA complexes were predicted on 15 November 2026 using the AlphaFold Server (AlphaFold 3, default parameters, automatic random seed, five diffusion samples per job) (16) under non-commercial research terms. PAX9 paired-domain constructs (residues 6–137) were modelled with the double-stranded recognition DNA duplex taken from the human PAX6 paired domain–DNA crystal structure (PDB 6PAX) (22). MSX1 homeodomain constructs (residues 166–237) were modelled with the recognition DNA duplex from the murine Msx-1 homeodomain–DNA crystal structure (PDB 1IG7) (23). Forty-one jobs were executed (two wild-type complexes and 39 pathogenic missense mutants). For each job, the top-ranked sample by `ranking_score` was selected, recording global interface predicted TM-score (ipTM), predicted TM-score (pTM), protein–DNA chain-pair ipTM, minimum inter-chain predicted aligned error (PAE), per-residue pLDDT, and steric clash flags (`has_clash`).

Residue-to-DNA distance was measured in the top-ranked wild-type complex as the minimum Euclidean distance between any heavy atom of the amino acid residue and any heavy atom of either DNA strand. Residues were classified as direct DNA contact (< 4.0 Å), proximal (4.0–8.0 Å), or distal (> 8.0 Å). Robustness of contact assignment was checked across all five wild-type diffusion samples. In mutant models, interface perturbation was quantified as the percentage change in heavy-atom protein–DNA pairs (< 4.0 Å) relative to the corresponding wild-type model.

### 2.3 Protein folding stability estimation

Changes in folding free energy upon substitution (ΔΔG, in kcal/mol) were predicted using ThermoMPNN (commit `370f76e`, default weights `thermoMPNN_default.pt`, PyTorch 2.5.1 on CPU) (17) applied to the isolated protein chain of the top-ranked wild-type AlphaFold 3 models (`estruturas_wt/`, without DNA). Full site-saturation mutagenesis was computed across all construct positions (2,508 single-amino-acid substitutions for PAX9; 1,368 for MSX1), verifying zero-based coordinate alignment against wild-type reference residues. Following the thermodynamic convention of Dieckhaus et al. (17) ($\Delta\Delta G_{\text{fold}} = \Delta G_{\text{fold,mut}} - \Delta G_{\text{fold,WT}}$, negating MegaScale $\Delta\Delta G_{\text{ML}}$ values), positive ΔΔG values denote destabilizing substitutions. A planned secondary stability analysis with DDMut could not be completed because the web server returned internal errors, including on its documentation example.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis and threshold stability evaluation

Categorical proportions were compared with two-sided Fisher's exact tests, and enrichment of unique DNA-contacting pathogenic positions against the paired-domain baseline (33% contacting residues) was assessed with exact binomial tests. Continuous distributions (ΔΔG, AlphaMissense scores, missing-tooth counts) were compared using the Mann–Whitney *U* statistic, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and two-sided *p*-values computed using `scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu` (exact when tie-free; asymptotic with tie and continuity correction when ties were present, verifying primary-script normal approximations). Rank associations were summarized with Spearman's $\rho$.

To combine mechanisms in *PAX9* (32 pathogenic vs 48 recurrent gnomAD AC ≥ 2 controls), a variant was classified as rule-positive if located at a wild-type DNA-contact residue (< 4.0 Å) or if its predicted ΔΔG met or exceeded a cut-off $c$. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV; 80 folds) selected $c$ on each 79-variant training split across $\{0, 0.25, \dots, 3.0\}$ kcal/mol by maximizing Youden's index ($J = \text{sensitivity} + \text{specificity} - 1$) and evaluated the held-out variant. Sensitivity analyses varied the contact cut-off (3.5, 4.0, 5.0 Å) and ΔΔG cut-off (0.5, 1.0, 1.5 kcal/mol). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Wilson's score method for sensitivity and specificity and both Simel's log-scale approximation (24) and stratified bootstrap resampling (20,000 iterations) for positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−). Because *MSX1* contained only seven pathogenic domain variants, LOOCV was not performed for *MSX1*; the *PAX9*-derived cut-off (1.5 kcal/mol) was transferred to *MSX1* descriptively. Given the overlap between population controls and domain `U` variants, VUS classification was stratified into three mutually exclusive subgroups: (i) ClinVar-only VUS absent from gnomAD, (ii) singleton gnomAD variants (AC = 1), and (iii) recurrent gnomAD calibration variants (AC ≥ 2). Analyses were executed in Python 3.12.10 (`numpy` 2.5.4, `scipy` 1.18.1, `matplotlib` 3.11.2).

### 2.5 Mechanistic and clinical severity comparisons

Predicted DNA-binding loss (rule-positive status) was compared against experimental DNA-binding results reported by Wang et al. (6) for eight PAX9 paired-domain mutants (verified directly against the published full-text Table 1 and Figure 3B legend of PMC2706687; electrophoretic mobility shift assay [EMSA] score ≤ 1 defined loss of binding). Because all eight mutants belonged to the *PAX9* pathogenic set, this comparison evaluated mechanistic concordance rather than independent classification accuracy. Clinical severity was analysed using the number of missing permanent teeth (excluding third molars, one summary value per *PAX9* variant) compiled by Intarak et al. (2), comparing missense mechanism classes against each other and against 25 null (frameshift/nonsense) *PAX9* variants.

### 2.6 Non-coding variant analysis with AlphaGenome and SpliceAI

Eighty-four single-gene non-coding or splice-region ClinVar variants in *PAX9* and *MSX1* with GRCh38 SPDI coordinates were retrieved. After excluding three coding/boundary indels (leaving 81 submitted variants) and three upstream intergenic *MSX1* entries returning no intragenic score, 78 variants had gene-restricted AlphaGenome 2 scores (7 Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic, 37 Benign/Likely benign, 34 VUS). One translational `start_lost` variant (*PAX9* `g.36662092G>A`, M1I) was excluded from splicing-specific comparisons (leaving 77 variants; 6 pathogenic, 37 benign, 34 VUS), of which 75 single-nucleotide variants also had precomputed SpliceAI Δ scores retrieved via the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor REST API (21) (two small deletions lacked precomputed SpliceAI entries).

Variants were scored in a 1 Mb genomic window using AlphaGenome 2 (`alphagenome` Python package v2.0.0, API v2, all folds; splice-site, splice-site usage, splice-junction, and RNA-seq scorers restricted to the target gene) and compared with AlphaGenome 1 quantile scores (API v1) (20). Because no odontogenic tissue is available among AlphaGenome's cell ontology conditions, predicted wild-type RNA-seq expression (99th percentile across 12 tracks) of *PAX9* and *MSX1* was benchmarked against *GAPDH* (positive control) and *ALB* (hepatocyte negative control) across four candidate conditions: mesenchymal stem cell (`CL:0000134`, selected as primary given developmental proximity to undifferentiated dental ectomesenchyme and higher predicted *PAX9* expression relative to *ALB*), cranial neural crest cell (`CL:0011012`), osteoblast (`UBERON:0002481_CL:0000062`), and tongue (`UBERON:0001723`). In compliance with tool terms of use, AlphaFold Server and AlphaGenome outputs were generated strictly for non-commercial research and must not be used for clinical or diagnostic decision-making.

### 2.7 Ethical considerations

This study was purely computational and secondary in nature: no biological specimens or DNA were collected, no new genetic sequencing or laboratory procedures were performed, and no human participants were recruited or contacted. Every data source was verified to contain strictly non-identifiable, variant-level or aggregate information: ClinVar records consist solely of public variant accessions and aggregate clinical assertions without patient identifiers; gnomAD v4 queries (`gnomad_r4`) provide only population-level allele counts and frequencies without individual-level genotypes; structural and sequence models rely exclusively on canonical reference sequences (UniProt P55771/P28360, GRCh38) and public Protein Data Bank coordinates (6PAX, 1IG7); and literature datasets (2, 3, 6) comprise published variant-level summary tables without personal or clinical-chart identifiers. Because only publicly accessible, fully anonymous, non-identifiable data were analysed, institutional ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required.

### 3.2 Insensitivity of AlphaFold 3 mutant complexes to point substitutions

Wild-type AlphaFold 3 models achieved high confidence across both protein–DNA interfaces: PAX9–DNA yielded ipTM = 0.86, pTM = 0.85, and 361 heavy-atom contacts across 44 interface residues (**Fig. 1A, 1C**), whereas MSX1–DNA yielded ipTM = 0.87, pTM = 0.88, and 179 contacts across 22 interface residues (**Fig. 1B**). Wild-type pLDDT at all 39 mutated positions ranged from 86.1 to 98.9. However, re-predicting each of the 39 pathogenic missense mutants with AlphaFold 3 produced virtually unchanged global confidence scores (ipTM 0.84–0.87; pTM 0.83–0.89; |Δ protein–DNA chain-pair ipTM| ≤ 0.035; zero clashes in all 41 jobs; **Table 1**, **Supplementary Table S1**). Heavy-atom protein–DNA contact counts varied only modestly (−5.8% to +9.1% in PAX9; −15.6% to +18.4% in MSX1). Critically, four PAX9 substitutions shown experimentally to abolish DNA binding in vitro (6)—L21P, R26W, R28P, and I87F—retained native-like predicted interfaces with slight contact increases (+9.1%, +0.3%, +0.6%, and +5.8%, respectively). Direct inspection of AlphaFold 3 mutant interfaces therefore failed to capture missense loss of function, motivating mechanistic decomposition using wild-type DNA geometry and ThermoMPNN folding stability.

### 3.3 Two complementary mechanisms in the PAX9 paired domain and MSX1 homeodomain

In the wild-type PAX9 paired-domain model, 13 of the 27 unique pathogenic missense positions contacted DNA (< 4.0 Å; 48.1% vs 33.0% domain-wide baseline; one-sided exact binomial *p* = 0.074; two-sided *p* = 0.103). Across the five AlphaFold 3 diffusion samples, contact assignment was invariant for 12 of these 13 sites, varying only at Val117 (3.9 Å in the top-ranked sample). Considered by variant (n = 32), 16 substitutions occurred at DNA-contact sites (< 4.0 Å), 8 were proximal (4.0–8.0 Å), and 8 were distal (> 8.0 Å) (**Fig. 1A**, **Fig. 2**).

Integrating ThermoMPNN ΔΔG estimates revealed a strong inverse relationship between DNA proximity and folding destabilization (**Fig. 2**). DNA-contacting *PAX9* variants displayed a median ΔΔG of 0.50 kcal/mol (0.498 unrounded; 0.495 from two-decimal Table 1 values; n = 16), indistinguishable from the saturation-mutagenesis background across all 2,508 PAX9 substitutions (median 0.56 kcal/mol), exemplified by N17K (0.15 kcal/mol), R44C (0.40 kcal/mol), S49L (0.46 kcal/mol), G51A/S/V (−0.14 to 0.23 kcal/mol), and R77Q/G (0.43 and 0.89 kcal/mol). Conversely, non-contact *PAX9* variants were markedly destabilizing, with medians of 1.84 kcal/mol among proximal variants (n = 8) and 1.81 kcal/mol among distal variants (n = 8; combined non-contact median 1.81 kcal/mol vs contact median 0.50 kcal/mol; Mann–Whitney AUC = 0.88, *p* = 0.00028), led by helix-breaking prolines and core substitutions such as R28P (3.26 kcal/mol), A108P (2.50 kcal/mol), L27P (2.45 kcal/mol), I56N (2.38 kcal/mol), and I29T (2.28 kcal/mol).

In the MSX1 homeodomain (n = 7), pathogenic variants had a median ΔΔG of 1.52 kcal/mol compared with 0.29 kcal/mol across all 1,368 homeodomain substitutions (AUC = 0.76), with L211R (2.23 kcal/mol), T180I (1.67 kcal/mol), L230P (1.54 kcal/mol), and R202P (1.52 kcal/mol) ranking in the top 16.2% of destabilizing changes, alongside DNA-contacting R202H (2.2 Å, 0.80 kcal/mol) (**Fig. 1B**, **Fig. 2**). Two *MSX1* substitutions—A225T (reported in autosomal recessive tooth agenesis; ΔΔG = 0.40 kcal/mol) and A227E (ΔΔG = 0.34 kcal/mol)—neither contacted DNA nor exceeded 1.5 kcal/mol.

### 3.4 Comparison of pathogenic variants with gnomAD v4 population controls

Whereas enrichment of *PAX9* contact sites over the uniform domain background did not reach statistical significance (*p* = 0.074), comparison against recurrent population variants observed in gnomAD v4 (AC ≥ 2, n = 48) demonstrated strong enrichment for both mechanisms (**Table 2**, **Fig. 3**). Pathogenic *PAX9* variants were three-fold more likely than recurrent gnomAD variants to alter a DNA-contacting residue (50.0% [16/32] vs 16.7% [8/48]; Fisher's exact *p* = 0.0025) and exhibited substantially higher destabilization (median ΔΔG 1.17 vs 0.34 kcal/mol; AUC = 0.77, Mann–Whitney *p* = 4.6×10⁻⁵; ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol in 40.6% [13/32] vs 4.2% [2/48]; **Fig. 3A, 3B**). Under the primary combined rule (DNA contact < 4.0 Å or ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol), 84.4% (27/32) of pathogenic *PAX9* variants were positive compared with 20.8% (10/48) of recurrent population controls (Fisher's exact *p* = 1.7×10⁻⁸; under ΔΔG ≥ 1.0 kcal/mol, 93.8% [30/32] vs 33.3% [16/48], *p* = 3.4×10⁻⁸). Consistent results were obtained against all gnomAD *PAX9* domain variants (AC ≥ 1, n = 124; **Table 2**). In *MSX1*, pathogenic variants were significantly more destabilizing than recurrent gnomAD controls (median ΔΔG 1.52 vs 0.21 kcal/mol; AUC = 0.88, Mann–Whitney *p* = 0.0021), whereas DNA-contact proportions did not differ significantly (28.6% [2/7] vs 20.7% [6/29]; *p* = 0.64). AlphaMissense achieved high rank discrimination (AUC = 0.87 in *PAX9* and 0.88 in *MSX1* vs AC ≥ 2 controls), but assigned extreme scores across almost the entire conserved domains, with control medians of 0.983–0.993 in *PAX9* and 0.940–0.983 in *MSX1* (**Table 2**).

### 3.5 Threshold stability in cross-validation and transfer to *MSX1*

Across all 80 LOOCV folds in *PAX9* (32 pathogenic vs 48 recurrent gnomAD AC ≥ 2 controls), maximizing Youden's index selected the identical cut-off of ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol in every fold. Because threshold selection was invariant across all 80 training splits, held-out LOOCV estimates equalled apparent performance on the calibration dataset: sensitivity 0.84 (27/32; 95% Wilson CI 0.68–0.93), specificity 0.79 (38/48; 95% Wilson CI 0.66–0.88), LR+ 4.05 (Simel 95% CI 2.29–7.17; bootstrap 95% CI 2.50–8.40), and LR− 0.20 (Simel 95% CI 0.09–0.45; bootstrap 95% CI 0.04–0.38). Varying the contact threshold (3.5–5.0 Å) and ΔΔG threshold (0.5–1.5 kcal/mol) showed predictable trade-offs between sensitivity (0.72–0.97) and specificity (0.54–0.83) (**Supplementary Table S2**). Applying the *PAX9*-derived 1.5 kcal/mol cut-off without re-fitting to *MSX1* (7 pathogenic vs 29 recurrent controls) yielded an apparent sensitivity of 0.71 (5/7; 95% Wilson CI 0.36–0.92), specificity of 0.79 (23/29; 95% Wilson CI 0.62–0.90), LR+ of 3.45 (Simel 95% CI 1.47–8.10), and LR− of 0.36 (Simel 95% CI 0.11–1.18).

### 3.6 Mechanistic concordance with published *PAX9* DNA-binding assays

Comparing the primary structural rule (< 4.0 Å or ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol) against in vitro EMSA measurements for eight *PAX9* paired-domain mutants characterized by Wang et al. (6) yielded concordance for 5 of 8 variants (**Table 3**). Four mutants with severe or complete experimental DNA-binding loss were correctly classified as positive—two via direct DNA contact (L21P, R26W) and two via core destabilization (R28P, I87F)—and G6R, which retained substantial DNA binding and caused the mildest clinical phenotype (three missing permanent teeth), was correctly classified as rule-negative (9.5 Å, ΔΔG = 0.44 kcal/mol). Among the three discordant variants, S43K (7.2 Å, reduced binding `+` in EMSA) had a predicted ΔΔG of 1.46 kcal/mol—just 0.04 kcal/mol below the 1.5 kcal/mol threshold—and became concordant (6/8) in a sensitivity analysis using ΔΔG ≥ 1.0 kcal/mol. The remaining two exceptions acted through functional mechanisms beyond monomeric DNA binding: G51S contacted DNA (3.8 Å, ΔΔG = −0.14 kcal/mol) and retained DNA binding in vitro while abolishing transcriptional synergy with MSX1 in a dominant-negative manner, whereas K91E was predicted to destabilize the domain (ΔΔG = 2.07 kcal/mol) yet retained EMSA binding while losing transactivation activity (6). Descriptively across these eight variants, ThermoMPNN ΔΔG correlated moderately with the structural stability change modelled by Wang et al. (6) ($\rho = 0.55$), whereas experimental DNA binding correlated inversely with missing-tooth count ($\rho = -0.72$).

### 3.7 Predicted mechanism versus clinical tooth-loss severity

Among 25 pathogenic *PAX9* paired-domain missense variants with permanent missing-tooth counts compiled by Intarak et al. (2), clinical severity did not differ between structural mechanisms (**Fig. 4**): under the 1.5 kcal/mol rule, median missing teeth (excluding third molars) were 9.5 for DNA-contact variants (n = 10), 11.0 for non-contact destabilizing variants (n = 11), and 7.0 for variants meeting neither criterion (n = 4; destabilizing vs contact AUC = 0.61, Mann–Whitney *p* = 0.42; under ΔΔG ≥ 1.0 kcal/mol, 10.0 vs 9.5, *p* = 0.60). ThermoMPNN ΔΔG showed weak rank correlation with missing-tooth count among missense variants ($\rho = 0.23$). By contrast, null *PAX9* variants (frameshift and nonsense, n = 25) caused significantly more severe oligodontia than paired-domain missense variants (median 15.0 vs 10.0 missing teeth; AUC = 0.77, *p* = 0.0009 (displayed as *p* = 0.001 in Fig. 4); **Fig. 4**).

### 3.8 Non-coding variant evaluation with AlphaGenome and SpliceAI

Benchmarking predicted wild-type transcription across candidate cell conditions in AlphaGenome 2 showed that mesenchymal stem cells (`CL:0000134`) expressed both *PAX9* (11.1× *ALB*) and *MSX1* (34.6× *ALB*), and osteoblasts (`UBERON:0002481_CL:0000062`) similarly expressed both genes (*PAX9* 6.8×, *MSX1* 54.8× *ALB*), whereas cranial neural crest cells (`CL:0011012`) did not express *PAX9* (0.7× *ALB*) despite *MSX1* expression (19.0× *ALB*). In tongue (`UBERON:0001723`), elevated background *ALB* signal (99th percentile 1.95) precluded ratio normalization.

Across all 78 scored ClinVar variants, maximum AlphaGenome 2 splicing score separated pathogenic from benign variants with AUC = 0.988. After excluding the *PAX9* `start_lost` variant (`g.36662092G>A`) to evaluate true non-coding/splice entries (n = 77; 6 pathogenic, 37 benign, 34 VUS), AlphaGenome 2 splice-site scoring, AlphaGenome 1 quantile scoring, and precomputed SpliceAI Δ scores (available for 75 single-nucleotide variants; 5 pathogenic, 36 benign, 34 VUS) each achieved perfect separation between curated pathogenic splice variants and benign controls (AUC = 1.000; **Fig. 5**), while AlphaGenome 2 RNA-seq scoring yielded AUC = 0.982 (0.94–0.99 across cell conditions; **Supplementary Table S3**). Thus, AlphaGenome provided no incremental discrimination over SpliceAI for splice-site classification.

Crucially, seven intronic ClinVar VUS were concordantly prioritized across mesenchymal stem cell, osteoblast, and tongue conditions in AlphaGenome 2, AlphaGenome 1 (quantile ≥ 0.9995), and SpliceAI (Δ score 0.40–1.00) (**Fig. 5**, **Supplementary Table S3**): two deep-proximal intronic *MSX1* substitutions—`NM_002448.3:c.470-9G>A` (VCV002734641; AlphaGenome 2 = 0.93, SpliceAI = 1.00) and `c.470-10C>A` (VCV004085302; AlphaGenome 2 = 0.92, SpliceAI = 1.00)—both predicted to create a cryptic splice acceptor upstream of exon 2, and five splice-region *PAX9* substitutions (`NM_001372076.1:c.772-2A>G`, `c.772-3C>G`, `c.771+1G>A`, `c.771+5G>C`, and `c.771+3G>T`). Three *PAX9* 3' untranslated region VUS (`c.*825T>G`, `c.*775C>T`, `c.*231T>G`) exceeded background only in tongue (SpliceAI 0–0.16) and were judged weak, tissue-restricted signals.

### 3.9 Exploratory structural characterization of domain VUS

Applying the primary structural rule (< 4.0 Å or ΔΔG ≥ 1.5 kcal/mol) descriptively across all `U`-labelled domain missense variants flagged 45 of 138 (32.6%) *PAX9* VUS and 23 of 72 (31.9%) *MSX1* VUS as rule-positive (**Supplementary Table S4**). Because `U` variants overlap with gnomAD controls, decomposing positive rates by population subgroup revealed a steep gradient in *PAX9*: rule-positive variants accounted for 71.4% (10/14) of ClinVar-only VUS absent from gnomAD (e.g., L106R [ΔΔG = 3.19 kcal/mol], L32P [3.08 kcal/mol], V83G [3.04 kcal/mol], R105P [2.35 kcal/mol], and R88P [1.85 kcal/mol]), 32.9% (25/76) of singleton gnomAD variants outside calibration (AC = 1; e.g., DNA-contacting R26Q [2.8 Å, ΔΔG = 1.80 kcal/mol], affecting the same arginine as pathogenic R26W), and 20.8% (10/48) of recurrent gnomAD AC ≥ 2 variants (matching the calibration false-positive rate by construction). Conversely, recurrent population variants with high AlphaMissense scores—such as D94E (AC = 37, ΔΔG = 0.36 kcal/mol, AlphaMissense = 0.988), Y115H (AC = 25, ΔΔG = 0.04 kcal/mol, AlphaMissense = 0.951), and E62K (AC = 10, ΔΔG = −0.25 kcal/mol, AlphaMissense = 0.9994)—were cleanly separated as rule-negative. In *MSX1*, rule-positive proportions across the three subgroups were 35.7% (5/14; e.g., W219R [3.4 Å, ΔΔG = 2.61 kcal/mol] and K228E [2.1 Å, ΔΔG = 0.24 kcal/mol]), 40.0% (12/30; e.g., A227P [ΔΔG = 2.08 kcal/mol]), and 21.4% (6/28; e.g., R224C [2.6 Å, ΔΔG = 0.94 kcal/mol]).

## 4. Discussion

This study demonstrates that most pathogenic missense substitutions in the PAX9 paired domain—and, more preliminary in sample size, the MSX1 homeodomain—disrupt odontogenic transcription factor function through two largely mutually exclusive biophysical mechanisms: direct removal of protein–DNA contacts or thermodynamic destabilization of the folded domain. These findings closely parallel the classic division between contact and structural p53 mutants (10, 11) and mirror the landmark FoldX study of the homologous PAX6 paired domain by Alibés et al. (12), in which 44 of 50 (88%) aniridia-associated missense mutations were energetically explained (37 involving folding stability, 6 DNA-binding affinity, and 9 DNA specificity, with overlap). Notably, our LOOCV-stabilized ThermoMPNN threshold of 1.5 kcal/mol converged closely with the 1.6 kcal/mol high-confidence FoldX threshold used in PAX6 (12) and aligns with approximately 1–1.5 times the reported root-mean-square error of ThermoMPNN across experimental benchmarks (0.71 kcal/mol on MegaScale and 1.51 kcal/mol on Fireprot) (17). Furthermore, benchmarking pathogenic substitutions against human population variants observed in gnomAD v4 within the exact same domain provides a more stringent neutral comparator than cross-species orthologue alignments (12), revealing significant enrichment of DNA-contact variants (*p* = 0.0025) that was masked when tested solely against the uniform geometric baseline of the paired domain (*p* = 0.074).

Structurally, DNA-contacting *PAX9* substitutions cluster within the N-terminal subdomain $\beta$-hairpin, recognition helices, and inter-subdomain linker (22), sparing intrinsic folding energy (median ΔΔG 0.50 kcal/mol) while eliminating electrostatic or hydrogen-bond interactions with the DNA duplex. By contrast, non-contact substitutions frequently introduce helix-disrupting prolines or polar/steric clashes into hydrophobic $\alpha$-helical cores (e.g., R28P, L27P, A108P, I56N; median ΔΔG 1.81 kcal/mol), consistent with impaired domain folding and secondary loss of DNA binding despite intact nuclear localization signals observed experimentally (6). Distinguishing these two mechanisms also carries conceptual relevance: whereas DNA-contact defects abolish direct sequence readout, purely destabilizing missense proteins retain an intact recognition surface and could, in principle, be amenable to pharmacological chaperone stabilization strategies explored in structural transcription factor mutants (11, 12).

Equally informative are the exceptions that delineate the boundaries of a two-feature monomeric model. In our comparison with Wang et al. (6), G51S preserved DNA binding in vitro yet caused severe oligodontia by abolishing synergistic co-activation of *Bmp4* and *Msx1* promoters with MSX1 in a dominant-negative fashion, while K91E retained DNA binding in EMSA despite predicted destabilization and failed at a subsequent transactivation step. In addition, S43K fell just 0.04 kcal/mol below the 1.5 kcal/mol cut-off (ΔΔG = 1.46 kcal/mol), illustrating the unavoidable imprecision of a hard threshold when predictor uncertainty is roughly 1 kcal/mol (17). Moreover, we did not model subtle shifts in DNA sequence specificity—such as position-weight-matrix alterations captured via FoldX binding logos in PAX6 (12)—and recent high-throughput protein-binding microarray studies of 92 rare homeodomain variants have shown that current computational predictors remain poorly sensitive to specificity-switching substitutions that preserve overall affinity (15).

A key methodological finding of our study is that AlphaFold 3 mutant complex predictions cannot be used directly to infer missense loss of DNA binding. Across all 39 pathogenic variants, ipTM scores and heavy-atom protein–DNA contact counts remained native-like, even for biochemical null alleles such as L21P and R28P. This extends to protein–DNA complexes the well-documented insensitivity of AlphaFold2 monomer predictions to destabilizing point mutations reported by Pak et al. (25). Because evolutionary couplings and template geometry anchor the global fold and docking pose, functional interpretation of missense variants in transcription factors should combine wild-type structural coordinates with dedicated thermodynamic predictors such as ThermoMPNN rather than relying on mutant ipTM shifts. By contrast, AlphaMissense (19) achieved strong rank ordering but exhibited pronounced score saturation across both conserved DNA-binding domains (control medians 0.94–0.99), limiting its specificity without domain-specific recalibration.

Consistent with observations in PAX6 (12), whether a *PAX9* missense allele acted via loss of DNA contact or domain destabilization did not predict the number of missing permanent teeth (*p* = 0.42). Both mechanisms converge on reduced functional transcriptional output in dental mesenchyme. However, paired-domain missense substitutions as a group caused significantly fewer missing teeth than frameshift or nonsense *PAX9* alleles (median 10.0 vs 15.0 teeth; *p* = 0.0009), supporting the hypothesis that many clinical missense alleles retain residual hypomorphic activity in vivo (2, 6).

To our knowledge, this work also represents the first evaluation of AlphaGenome (20) in dental genetics. On ClinVar splice-region variants, AlphaGenome 1 and 2 matched SpliceAI (21) (AUC = 1.00), adding no extra classification power for canonical or near-splice variants—an expected finding given the small pathogenic sample (n = 5–6) dominated by high-impact splice-site alleles. Nevertheless, concordant predictions across mesenchymal stem cells, osteoblasts, tongue, and SpliceAI highlighted seven intronic VUS for functional follow-up, most notably *MSX1* `c.470-9G>A` and `c.470-10C>A`, which are predicted to generate a strong cryptic acceptor upstream of exon 2. Critically, checking predicted baseline expression prevented misinterpretation in cranial neural crest cells, where *PAX9* was transcriptionally silent (0.7× *ALB*). While AlphaGenome's theoretical advantage over SpliceAI lies in distal cis-regulatory expression modelling, the absence of confirmed pathogenic regulatory variants in *PAX9* or *MSX1* and the lack of primary tooth-germ or dental-papilla tracks among AlphaGenome's ~2,800 conditions currently preclude validation of regulatory expression scores in odontogenesis.

Under the ClinGen Bayesian calibration framework for ACMG/AMP computational criteria PP3 and BP4 (26) and recent structure-informed BRCA1 calibrations (27), the *PAX9* likelihood ratios estimated here (LR+ = 4.05, LR− = 0.20) fall near the nominal supporting-to-moderate evidence range, with rule-negative status providing useful benign-supporting discrimination for recurrent population substitutions where sequence conservation models saturate. In practice, ClinVar-only VUS absent from gnomAD showed a 71.4% rule-positive rate in *PAX9*, prioritizing substitutions such as L106R, L32P, V83G, and R26Q for experimental investigation. Nevertheless, our rules and likelihood ratios are strictly research-level findings and must not be applied directly in clinical diagnostic reporting.

Twelve specific limitations temper our conclusions: (i) no independent external test cohort of pathogenic and benign variants exists—LOOCV demonstrates only that the 1.5 kcal/mol cut-off is stable within the 80-variant *PAX9* calibration set, and out-of-sample LOOCV metrics equal apparent performance because 1.5 kcal/mol was selected in every fold; (ii) recurrent gnomAD controls (AC ≥ 2) overlap completely with a subset of domain `U` variants (48/138 in *PAX9*, 28/72 in *MSX1*), making LR estimates optimistic if applied back to calibration controls rather than to independent ClinVar-only or singleton subsets (conversely, rare undiagnosed hypodontia alleles within gnomAD make case–control enrichment conservative); (iii) ΔΔG values rely on a single tool (ThermoMPNN, typical error ≈1 kcal/mol) computed on apo wild-type protein chains without DNA stabilization; (iv) wild-type DNA contact coordinates derive from AlphaFold 3 models built with heterologous DNA duplexes borrowed from PAX6 (PDB 6PAX) and murine Msx-1 (PDB 1IG7) rather than experimental human PAX9–DNA structures, and the exact minor subversion of `gnomad_r4` (v4.0 vs v4.1) was not logged by the GraphQL endpoint; (v) the *MSX1* pathogenic sample is very small (n = 7), precluding cross-validation and leaving its LR− confidence interval crossing unity (0.11–1.18); (vi) functional concordance relied on a single experimental study of eight *PAX9* mutants (6) that were all part of the pathogenic calibration set; (vii) clinical severity relied on a single literature-derived summary tooth count per variant across heterogeneous family reports with known intrafamilial variable expressivity (2); (viii) ClinVar assertions may incorporate computational PP3 evidence (often from single submitters or without assertion criteria), and AlphaMissense comparisons against gnomAD are partially circular; (ix) no wet-laboratory validation (such as EMSA, thermal shift, or minigene splicing assays) was performed; (x) AlphaGenome lacks odontogenic cell types; (xi) sample sizes yield wide 95% confidence intervals (e.g., *PAX9* sensitivity 0.68–0.93, specificity 0.66–0.88); and (xii) study design choices—including the 4.0 Å contact cut-off, OR-rule structure, AC ≥ 2 control definition, and progression from an initial 1.0 kcal/mol exploratory cut-off to cross-validated 1.5 kcal/mol—were established with access to the dataset outside cross-validation.

In conclusion, pathogenic missense variants in the PAX9 paired domain act predominantly through two complementary mechanisms—loss of direct DNA contact or domain destabilization—that cannot be detected from mutant AlphaFold 3 interface metrics alone but are effectively captured by combining wild-type protein–DNA geometry with ThermoMPNN folding stability. Alongside joint AlphaGenome and SpliceAI prioritization of intronic splice candidates, this structure-based framework offers transparent, hypothesis-generating prioritization to guide functional assays in non-syndromic tooth agenesis.
